Part one

Where your bills have been going

Some of this came from Broomfield. Some of it came from the state. All of it arrives at the same address.

In Broomfield

2025In effect

An average bill went from $80.05 to $119.90

Ordinances 2253 and 2255, adopted October 22, 2024, took effect January 1, 2025. Those are the city’s own figures for an average single family bill, water, sewer and stormwater together. An increase of $39.85 a month.

Of that, $11 was a stormwater charge that had never been billed before. The water base fee went from $16.93 to $36.91.

2026In effect

Year two, up 15 percent

Ordinance 2286, adopted October 14, 2025, took effect January 1, 2026. The city’s outlook puts the average bill at $138.05.

City of Broomfield, Ordinance 2286; utility rate FAQ four year outlook.
2028Projected

The plan ends at $158.22

The city publishes a four year outlook: $119.90, then $138.05, then $148.11, then $158.22. Seven percent in 2027 and again in 2028. Staff recommended staying on that plan at the July 21, 2026 study session.

Read the whole line and an average monthly bill roughly doubles from where it stood in 2024. The later years are projections, not adopted rates.

What the city says back

Broomfield’s average utility bill was the lowest among comparable neighboring communities before any of this, and the city expects it will still not be the highest afterward. Utilities here are enterprise funds, which by law cannot be paid for out of tax revenue.

The city also objects to the shorthand, writing that calling it a 50 percent increase “generally is misleading” because it combines three utilities. That is why the dollars are used above instead. If the bill is a hardship, Broomfield runs a Utility Rate Assistance Fund.

From the state

2024In effect

Your gas bill went up

Xcel asked for about $171 million. State regulators approved $130.76 million, about $4.57 a month on an average residential bill, roughly 7.7 percent. Rates were effective November 5, 2024, though the increase was deferred and did not reach bills until February 2025, when the Winter Storm Uri charge came off.

Colorado Public Utilities Commission, Proceeding 24AL-0049G, deliberations concluded October 25, 2024.
2026In effect

Your electric bill went up last month

Xcel asked for about $356 million. A settlement would have made it $225 million. The commission rejected that and approved roughly $157 million, a 4.77 percent residential increase, about $5.00 a month. New rates took effect August 29.

Worth saying plainly: regulators cut the request by more than half. The bill still went up, from about $104.91 to $109.92 for an average household.

One commissioner put the longer worry on the record, noting that Xcel had tripled its capital spending since 2021 while electricity sales declined: “if capital spending and rates continue to grow at levels considerably faster than inflation, electricity may simply become unaffordable for many customers.”

Colorado Public Utilities Commission, Proceeding 25AL-0494E, decision August 20, 2026.
2026In effect

The delivery fee rose again

Colorado charges a flat fee on deliveries by motor vehicle that include at least one item subject to sales tax. It was 27 cents when it started in 2022 and is 31 cents as of July 1, 2026. It has moved both directions in between, but the trend is up.

It is charged once per delivery no matter how many items are in the box. The legislature created it in 2021 and wrote in an annual inflation adjustment, so the rate now moves every July without a further vote. Businesses under $500,000 in prior year sales are exempt, and a retailer may absorb the fee rather than pass it on.

Colorado Department of Revenue, retail delivery fee rate schedule and guidance; SB21-260.

The paid leave deduction

Not an increase, a permanent line. The state paid family leave program takes 0.44 percent of your wages, up to $184,500 in earnings. On a $65,000 salary that is about $286 a year, every year, unless your employer chooses to cover it. Employers with ten or more workers pay a matching 0.44 percent; smaller employers are exempt from the match.

Colorado voters created the program in 2020, and it pays wage replacement to workers who take leave for a new child or a serious illness. The rate is recalculated by the state each year under a statutory formula and was trimmed from 0.90 to 0.88 percent for 2026.

Colorado Family and Medical Leave Insurance Division, 2026 premium rate and small business rules.
Not one of these was a big number on its own.
They arrive at the same address, in the same month, out of the same paycheck. That is what makes them add up.
Part two

What is on your ballot in November

One Broomfield question and a statewide slate. Not every one of these lands on a household bill, and we say so where it does not. All of them are open until the envelopes come back.

Broomfield questions

Your utility bill

The Xcel franchise question

A franchise agreement running from January 1, 2028 through December 31, 2048. Twenty years. The 3 percent franchise fee does not change, and Xcel continues contributing 1 percent of electric gross revenue toward the underground conversion program.

Xcel collects the franchise fee from customers and remits all of it to the city, so it is a pass-through. It appears as a line on Xcel bills, gas as well as electric. Households served by United Power rather than Xcel would not see this particular fee.

Referred on second reading July 7, 2026. The fee is not going up. What is on the table is locking the arrangement in until 2048.

Permanent by design

The lodging tax question

Resolution 2026-105 refers a lodging tax increase from 1.6 percent to 6 percent, dedicated to the Broomfield Housing Alliance. The tax applies to hotels, motels and short term rentals. It raises about $900,000 a year now and would raise about $3.3 million. The stated reason for the timing is that inclusionary housing revenue is projected to reach zero at the end of 2027.

Two things to be straight about. The 1.6 percent rate has stood since voters approved it in 1997, and even at 6 percent Broomfield would sit below Thornton, Westminster, Boulder, Aurora and Wheat Ridge, which run 7 to 10 percent. And visitors pay most of a lodging tax, though residents booking a room here pay it too.

The reason to read this one closely is that it is built to be permanent. Asked about a sunset, the City and County Manager answered on the record: “You can’t sunset a tax if you have already taken bonds out on those funds.” She explained that lenders require a sustainable revenue stream over 25 to 30 years, which is what makes a sunset difficult.

That is staff being candid, not an accusation, and it is the whole point. One election, and it stays. Referred to the November 3 ballot on August 11, 2026.

Broomfield expects three local questions this November. The third is a district attorney term limit question, which extends the maximum from eight years to twelve. It carries no cost to a household, so it is not broken out here. Broomfield publishes its own local voter guide rather than relying on the state Blue Book, and it will carry all three.

Statewide measures

The income tax

Initiative 195

Would end Colorado’s single flat income tax rate and replace it with six brackets. Rates below $100,000 come down. The top rate reaches 8.40 percent on income over $1 million.

Start with the part that cuts against us, because you will find it anyway. State analysts put the break-even near $510,000 of income. Below that, most filers would owe modestly less on next year’s return.

The case against it is not next year’s return. It is that the flat rate is the protection. Colorado’s constitution requires one rate for everyone, so a rate increase has to be argued in front of every voter at once. Brackets end that. A bracket threshold is a number a future legislature can lower. A constitutional provision is not.

The revenue is also built on a narrow and highly mobile group of filers. Estimates run from about $2 billion a year to the state’s own maximum figure of $2.7 billion, which is a wide spread for a budget to depend on, and the people it depends on are the ones most able to file from somewhere else.

Signatures went in on August 3, the fewest of any measure headed for this ballot, and because it amends the constitution it also needs 2 percent of registered voters in every one of the 35 senate districts. Certification was still pending when this page was written. Check whether it made your ballot.

The other half of that fight

Initiative 232

The answer to 195 on the same ballot. Would lock Colorado’s income tax rate at 4.40 percent, where it already sits, starting in 2027.

State analysts scored its own effect on revenue at zero, and that is honest. It changes nothing on any return by itself. What it changes is how much room a future legislature has to move the rate.

Certified August 20 with 184,454 signatures. Passes on a simple majority. If both this and 195 pass, the one with more yes votes controls wherever the two conflict. Exactly how the rest would fit together is genuinely disputed.

Your TABOR refund

Two refund questions

Separate measures, same mechanism. Neither raises a tax rate. Both let the state keep money it would otherwise send back to taxpayers.

Initiative 308

Exempts the sales tax already collected on sporting goods from TABOR, roughly $175 to $180 million a year, and routes it to a new conservation and wildfire fund. Legislative analysts say it reduces refunds by about the same amount.

Proposition NN

Senate Bill 26-135, referred by the legislature on a party line vote. Lets the state keep revenue above the TABOR cap for K-12 schools and children’s programs.

This is the larger of the two by far. The final fiscal note projects refunds of $329.9 million in 2026-27 and $521.0 million in 2027-28 under current law, and expects this measure to eliminate them.

Neither does anything in a year the state collects under the cap. Colorado voters have turned down every attempt to lift that cap in the last decade, most recently Proposition HH in 2023.

What you pay to drive

Initiative 175

Would require the vehicle and fuel taxes you already pay to go to roads, bridges and the State Patrol rather than into the general pot.

A companion law signed in June would temporarily cut fuel taxes and registration fees from January 2027 through June 2030, but only if this measure passes.

Certified June 23. A redirection of money already collected, not a new tax. Whatever it moves to roads has to come out of something else.

Heating and cooking

Initiative 177

A constitutional amendment declaring a right to buy natural gas for home heating and cooking, and a right for utilities to sell it.

It sets no rate and collects nothing, so it will not lower a bill. Its effect would be to limit how far the state or a city can go in restricting gas hookups and appliances.

Certified in August. As a constitutional amendment it needs 55 percent to pass, not a simple majority.

Citizen initiatives are listed here by initiative number, which is how the state tracks them until certification. Colorado assigns the Amendment letters and Proposition numbers in early September, so those will read differently on your ballot. Measures referred by the legislature already have theirs, which is why Proposition NN appears by name. Broomfield question numbers and certified titles are not final until the county sets the ballot. Read the title in front of you, and check any figure on this page against the record yourself.

None of it is decided yet

Whoever mails the ballot back decides it.

  1. Your ballot arrives in the mail in October. Every active registered voter in Colorado gets one.
  2. Fill it out at the kitchen table, with the ballot questions in front of you.
  3. Sign the envelope. An unsigned envelope does not count.
  4. Drop it in any Broomfield drop box, or put it back in the mail. Ten minutes.
