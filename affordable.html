Part one

Colorado did not get expensive by accident

Some of what you pay was decided at the Capitol, on purpose, and in a way that never required your permission.

More than a third of Colorado households now spend at least 30 percent of their income on housing.
35.1 percent here against 33.0 percent nationally. A decade ago Colorado was better than the national average, not worse. Census American Community Survey, 2024.

What the legislature added to your bills

Every
gallonRising

The road usage fee

A new per gallon fee stacked on top of the 22 cent state gas tax. It started at 2 cents in April 2023 and is 6 cents today. It climbs to 7 cents next year and 8 cents in 2028, then rises with inflation after that.

Senate Bill 21-260; C.R.S. 43-4-217. Passed 20 to 15 in the Senate, 41 to 24 in the House.
Every
deliveryRising

The retail delivery fee

Charged once on any delivery to your door that contains something taxable, no matter how small the order. It began at 27 cents in 2022 and rose to 31 cents on July 1. It is indexed, so it moves every July without another vote.

Senate Bill 21-260 created the fee in 2021. Senate Bill 23-143 added an exemption in 2023 for sellers with $500,000 or less in Colorado sales, so small shops do not charge it and large retailers do. C.R.S. 43-4-218; Colorado Department of Revenue rate schedule.
Every
bagIn effect

The carryout bag fee

10 cents per paper bag since January 2024, with plastic carryout bags banned outright. The store keeps 40 percent and the local government takes 60. Small on its own, which is rather the point.

House Bill 21-1162. Waived for SNAP and WIC participants.
Every
EVRising

Electric vehicle fees

A registration fee plus a road usage equalization fee, $76.05 a year now, scheduled to reach $167.70 by 2032.

Senate Bill 21-260; Colorado Department of Transportation, presentation to the Transportation Legislation Review Committee, September 2025.

The part worth understanding

No
voteBy design

A tax needs your permission. A fee does not.

None of the charges above went to the ballot. Each was written as an enterprise fee, and under the Taxpayer’s Bill of Rights an enterprise sits outside the limits and outside the vote that any new tax would require.

This is legal. The Court of Appeals upheld the structure in May 2025. Legal is not the same as accountable, and the reason to route money this way is that the alternative is asking.

Being fair about it: voters closed part of the gap themselves in 2020, when Proposition 117 began requiring a vote on any new enterprise expected to collect more than $100 million in five years. The ten enterprises created since then come to roughly $98 million between them. The door is narrower than it was. It is not shut.

Colo. Const. art. X, sec. 20; C.R.S. 24-77-108; Americans for Prosperity v. State, 2025 COA 46.
Last year more people left Colorado for other states than moved here from them.
A net loss of 12,100 residents to other states in the year ending June 30, 2025. It is the first time Colorado has gone negative since 2004. Colorado State Demography Office, January 2026.

Part three

The measures that reach your wallet

Fourteen statewide measures are on the ballot this year, plus a regional rail question. These are the ones about money. Our recommendation is on each card, and the reasoning for all of them is in the voter guide.

Vote No The income tax

Amendment 87

Ends Colorado’s single flat 4.4 percent income tax and replaces it with six brackets running from 3.7 to 8.4 percent.

Start with the part that cuts against us, because you will find it anyway. State analysts put the break even near $510,000 of income. Below that, most filers would owe modestly less next year.

The case against it is not next year’s return. The flat rate is the protection. One rate for everyone means a rate increase has to be argued in front of every voter at once. Brackets end that, and a bracket threshold is a number a future legislature can lower. A constitutional provision is not.

The official ballot title puts the increase at $2.7 billion a year, raised from a small and highly mobile group of filers.

Vote Yes The other half of that fight

Proposition 136

The answer to Amendment 87 on the same ballot. Locks Colorado’s income tax rate at 4.40 percent, where it already sits, starting in 2027.

State analysts scored its own effect on revenue at zero, and that is honest. It changes nothing on any return by itself. What it changes is how much room a future legislature has to move the rate.

If both this and Amendment 87 pass, the one with more yes votes controls wherever the two conflict.

Vote No Your TABOR refund

Proposition NN

Lets the state keep revenue above the TABOR cap for K-12 schools and children’s programs instead of refunding it.

The final fiscal note projects refunds of $329.9 million in 2026-27 and $521.0 million in 2027-28 under current law, and expects this measure to eliminate them.

Referred by the legislature on a party line vote. No Republican voted yes.

Vote No Your TABOR refund

Proposition 137

Takes the sales tax already collected on sporting goods out from under TABOR, roughly $175 to $180 million a year, and routes it to a new conservation and wildfire fund.

It raises no rate. Legislative analysts say it reduces refunds by about the same amount it keeps.

Same mechanism as Proposition NN, smaller number.

Vote No A new sales tax

Ballot Issue 7A

Broomfield sits inside the Front Range Passenger Rail District, so this is on your ballot. It creates a 0.333 percent sales and use tax, about 33 cents on every $100 you spend, to build and run passenger rail from Pueblo to Fort Collins.

The certified title puts it at $295 million a year once fully collected, plus $580 million in borrowing with a repayment cost of $785 million. Groceries, gasoline, residential gas and electricity and prescriptions are exempt.

There is no end date in the question. It is the single largest tax increase on this ballot.

Vote Yes Heating and cooking

Amendment 82

Writes into the constitution a right to buy natural gas for home heating and cooking, and a right for utilities to sell it.

It sets no rate and collects nothing, so it will not lower a bill by itself. What it does is limit how far the state or a city can go in restricting gas hookups and appliances.

As a constitutional amendment it needs 55 percent to pass, not a simple majority.

Initiative 175, the road funding measure, was withdrawn by its own proponents on September 1 and is not on any ballot. The Secretary of State certified the final ballot on September 4, which is when these measures received the numbers above. Nine other statewide measures are on your ballot that do not turn on household costs, and the voter guide covers every one of them.
