Council Looks at Homes on Interlocken Office Land,
and a New Housing Bill for Taxpayers.

A Watchdog statement from the Broomfield County Republicans on the June 16, 2026 City Council study session.

FOR IMMEDIATE RELEASE · Broomfield, Colorado · June 17, 2026

At a study session on Tuesday, June 16, the Broomfield City Council took no binding votes, but it set direction on two items that should matter to every taxpayer. The first was an early look at turning office and commercial land in the Interlocken business park into housing. The second was a Housing Division update that asks the city to put about $640,000 of general fund money toward housing programs starting in the 2027 budget. Study sessions are where the direction gets set before the binding votes come later, so this is the time for neighbors to pay attention. The Broomfield County Republicans give Council credit where it earned it, and we flag the items that deserve a closer look.

What follows describes what Council actually did, item by item, followed by our response. Where we quote a council member, the words are taken from the official meeting video and its captions, and we point to the moment in the meeting so any neighbor can listen and judge for themselves. Captions are auto-generated and can include small errors, so we encourage residents to confirm against the recording.

An early look at adding homes to the Interlocken business park

Under a concept review, Council heard a proposal to amend the Interlocken Planned Unit Development to add residential at 100 and 115 Edgeview Drive, two parcels now set aside for office and commercial use. A concept review is an informal, early conversation. No vote was taken, no rezoning was approved, and nothing was decided. Council gave a potential applicant feedback on whether the idea is worth pursuing and what concerns would have to be answered first, including the parcels' location near the airport and the city's affordable housing requirements.

We support private property rights and a free market in housing, and a property owner is within his rights to ask. But Interlocken is one of Broomfield's premier business parks and a real part of the commercial tax base and the local job base. When commercial and office land is converted to rooftops, the city trades away the sales tax, the employers, and the daytime economy that help hold down the property taxes families pay. That is a real cost, and it should be counted before anyone gets excited about more density. We also want any housing affordability strings on a project to be clear and honest up front, not bolted on later. Council Member Paloma Delgadillo (Ward 2), who is up for election in 2027, pressed staff on price and who the homes would actually serve:

“it is really helpful to know because it gives us a sense as to whether this would be an accessible product for people.”

Council Member Paloma Delgadillo, June 16, 2026, concept review for the Interlocken PUD residential amendment (official meeting video, approx. 42:40).

To her credit, Council Member Laurie Anderson (Ward 4), who is also up for election in 2027, was cautious about the rezoning itself and said residents should be heard before anything moves:

“I am not in favor of rezoning based on the location of the airport, and I also think it would be incredibly wise to go out and really listen to the residents in Camden and that are already expressing differing views.”

Council Member Laurie Anderson, June 16, 2026, concept review for the Interlocken PUD residential amendment (official meeting video, approx. 1:42:30).

That is the right instinct, and we thank her for it. Putting housing next to an active airport raises real questions about noise, safety, and disclosures to future renters and buyers, and listening to the neighbors who already live nearby is exactly what residents should expect. If this concept ever comes back as a real application, we will be watching to see that the commercial tax base, the airport concerns, and the neighbors' voices all get weighed honestly.

A new general fund bill for housing programs

Under the study session, the Housing Division gave its 2026 update on programs, initiatives, and how they are paid for. The headline for taxpayers is the funding plan. Staff proposed that, starting with the 2027 budget, the city shift about $640,000 of housing program costs onto the general fund so that the city's Housing Gap Fund can be reserved for development and land acquisition. No money was appropriated that night. This was a presentation and a request for direction, and the real decision would come during the 2027 budget.

Six hundred forty thousand dollars a year is real money, and the general fund is the same account that pays for police, roads, parks, and the basics residents count on. The city's own finance staff have called the budget fragile. Before Council moves $640,000 of recurring cost onto the general fund, taxpayers deserve plain answers: which programs the money funds, how many Broomfield residents it actually helps, what results the city expects, and whether the spending sunsets or simply becomes permanent. We are glad several members asked sharp questions about where the dollars come from and how outside funds are used, and we want that scrutiny to carry all the way through the budget vote. Helping neighbors is a worthy goal. Doing it without a clear price tag and a clear measure of results is how a fragile budget gets more fragile.

Also that evening

Before the public study session, Council met in a closed executive session at 6:15 p.m. in the Council Conference Room. By its nature that meeting is not public, so we do not speculate about what was discussed. We simply note it happened and that the public's business should be done in public whenever the law allows.

A statement from the Chair

“Nothing was final on Tuesday, and that is exactly the point,” said Heather Stidham, Chair of the Broomfield County Republicans. “These early conversations are where a council shows you its instincts, and what we saw was a reach for more rooftops and more spending before anyone answered the plain questions a family would ask at the kitchen table. Laurie Anderson earned real credit for saying slow down and talk to the neighbors first, and we hope her colleagues carry that same patience into the budget and into any Interlocken application that follows. Broomfield can welcome employers and homeowners both. We just cannot keep treating our job centers and our savings like spare change.”

How residents can stay involved

Council meetings and study sessions are held most Tuesdays at 6 p.m. at One DesCombes Drive, and meetings are streamed online. Agendas, packets, and recordings are posted at the city's agenda portal. Because these items came up at a study session, the binding decisions are still ahead, on the Interlocken concept if it returns as an application and on the housing funding plan when the 2027 budget is set. This is the time to watch, show up, and weigh in.
